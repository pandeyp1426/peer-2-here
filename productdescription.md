Peer-2-Here 

Project Description 

Team members: Pradeep Pandey, Chloe Ferguson, Caleb Bare 
Human Computer Interaction | September 28th, 2026 

Vision Statement 

We propose Peer-2-Here as a social space where college students can find company for everyday activities and build friendships through shared experiences. Our initial design focuses on students within the University of Wisconsin–Stout community, including new students, commuters, and students whose schedules do not always match those of their friends. Students will be able to turn a free afternoon, a meal, or a study break into an opportunity to connect with someone who shares their interests. 

Our goal is to make participation feel approachable and voluntary. Students should be able to understand an invitation, decide whether an activity suits them, and control what they share before committing. Through accessible desktop and phone experiences, they will be able to organize small gatherings and maintain connections at their own pace. Over time, students could develop familiar circles through repeated activities, giving them a reason to return whenever they want company or have a plan to share. 

Description of the System 

Purpose and everyday use 

Students will use Peer-2-Here to organize and join casual activities such as lunch, study sessions, walks, or pickup football. For example, a student who finishes class and wants to get food might find an invitation saying, “Getting lunch at the student center around 1:30. Two more people are welcome.” The student can review the meeting details and participants, ask whether the group plans to stay for an hour, and join if the plan fits their schedule. They can later return to the activity to check for changes or withdraw if necessary. 

Students will have a practical reason to participate: they can find company for a specific activity without already belonging to a large social group. Newcomers can learn about others through their profiles and conversations, while returning participants can reconnect through another shared plan. The main social interaction will center on invitations and activity discussions. Students can move from expressing an interest to agreeing on a real gathering, with clear information about what participation involves. 

Sharing plans and interacting with others 

After creating an account, students can use a display name and optional interests to introduce themselves. Organizers can describe an activity and specify its date, start and end times, meeting place, expected cost, and maximum number of participants. They can include relevant details such as whether beginners are welcome, what to bring, and known venue accessibility information. These details help other students judge whether the activity fits their abilities, comfort, and available time. 

Organizers can share an invitation with the registered campus community or selected registered students. Before publishing, they can review the audience and the information they are sharing. Students who can access the invitation can read its discussion, ask questions, and see the organizer’s answers. Keeping questions with the activity lets participants find useful answers without searching through unrelated conversations. Contributors will understand that their questions and replies are visible to the invitation’s audience. 

An invitation allows a student to consider an activity; the student only becomes a participant after choosing to join and receiving confirmation. In this version, eligible students can join directly while spaces remain. When an activity is full, they can recognize that it is unavailable and look for another option. Participants can review their joined activities and recent updates, while organizers can see who has joined. Organizers can revise the plan or cancel it, and participants can identify the current details and any cancellation before leaving to attend. 

Desktop and phone use 

Students will be able to complete all three main tasks on both devices. On a desktop, they can use a larger viewing area to compare activities, review longer discussions, and organize plans alongside their own schedules. A keyboard supports detailed descriptions and responses. On a phone, students may use the site during a short break or just before a meeting. They will be able to work through information in shorter steps, check essential details, and respond to changes quickly. Consistent language and participation states will help students move between devices without relearning the tasks. 

Accessibility and user control 

Students with different visual, motor, and cognitive needs should be able to read, create, and respond to invitations. Our design goals include readable text that can be enlarged, sufficient contrast, clearly labeled inputs, keyboard access, and meaningful information for screen readers. Students should receive understandable feedback when they complete an action or need to correct an error. They should be able to understand an activity’s status through text, without relying on color alone, and use comfortably sized touch controls on phones. Essential event details will be available as text rather than only in a poster image. 

Students will control whether they join or leave an activity and which audience can see a plan they organize. An invitation will not automatically commit its recipient to attending. These choices support participation without unnecessary pressure. Organizers can describe known access conditions at a venue, and students can ask about their needs before joining; those descriptions will not represent independently verified guarantees of physical accessibility. 

Scope of this version 

The first version will support account access, finding and joining activities, and organizing activities, including their associated questions, replies, and updates. Students will not be able to purchase tickets, transfer money, reserve a venue, or track another person’s live location. They will arrange any payments or venue reservations separately. Separate private messaging, formal friend groups, and recurring event schedules are possible future extensions. The project will focus on designing and evaluating the three core experiences across devices; successful participation and attendance in the real world cannot be guaranteed. 

Tasks Supported in This Version 

Our three-person group will support the following three user tasks in both interfaces. Posting, discussing, updating, and withdrawing from activities are steps within these tasks. 

Task 1 Create an account or log in 

Action and purpose. A student wants to join the community or return to their existing plans. A new student can choose a username and password, provide a display name, and review the account details before completing registration. Optional interests can be added later. A returning student can log in with their username and password and access the activities they organize or have joined. If required information is missing or credentials are incorrect, the student can understand the problem and try again without unnecessarily repeating completed steps. Account access supports continuity and helps participants recognize the people with whom they are making plans. 

Desktop experience. Students can review the account requirements together and use a keyboard to enter and check their information. They can move through the process using the keyboard alone. This suits a deliberate first visit when they have time to introduce themselves. 

Phone experience. Students can complete the same registration or login process in short, clearly explained steps. They can use password autofill or paste credentials, reducing typing effort and errors on a small screen. They can defer optional profile information and still complete the task. Both interfaces will use the same account requirements and clear success or error feedback. 

Task 2 Find and join a suitable activity 

Action and purpose. A student wants company for an activity that fits their interests, availability, and budget. They can look for suitable invitations by activity type, date, time, or cost, then review a promising plan’s meeting place, duration, available spaces, and participants. They can read existing questions and ask their own before deciding. After choosing to join, they can confirm their participation, return to the current details and discussion, or withdraw if their circumstances change. This task turns a general wish to socialize into a specific plan the student can evaluate and commit to. 

Desktop experience. Students can compare several invitations and review fuller descriptions and conversations with fewer changes of context. This supports planning ahead, such as choosing an activity for a free evening later in the week. They can type a detailed question when they need clarification. 

Phone experience. Students can narrow their choices using the same criteria, then review one activity at a time with its practical details easy to find. Short questions and quick access to joined plans will support use between classes or near the meeting time. They can still read the complete discussion, join, and withdraw. Clear confirmation will help them distinguish browsing an invitation from actually joining it. 

Task 3 Organize an activity with others 

Action and purpose. A student wants to bring people together around a plan. They can describe the activity, enter practical details, set a participant limit, and choose the invitation’s audience. Before publishing, they can review and correct the information. Once the invitation is shared, they can review participants, answer questions, and communicate revised arrangements. If they cancel, they can make that decision clear to participants. This task gives students a way to initiate social opportunities and keep everyone working from the same plan. 

Desktop experience. Organizers can review the activity details and audience together, write longer descriptions, and manage participant questions while planning ahead. The larger workspace will support checking several details before publishing or making a substantial revision. 

Phone experience. Organizers can create the same invitation through shorter stages, with a review of the complete plan before publishing. They can also answer a question or update a meeting place while away from their computer. On either device, they can review who will receive the invitation or update and receive confirmation after publishing changes. These checks help prevent accidental sharing, duplicate posts, and uncertainty about whether participants have access to the revised plan.
