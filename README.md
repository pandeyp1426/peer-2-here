# peer-2-here
Peer-2-Here 

User Scenarios 

Team members: Pradeep Pandey, Chloe Ferguson, Caleb Bare 
 Human Computer Interaction | September 28th, 2026 

User 1 

Zaria, She/Her, 18 

Zaria is an incoming freshman in the Computer Science program at UW-Stout and hasn’t met anyone on campus yet. She wants to make friends to study with but doesn’t know where to start. Her Stout issued laptop is the first laptop she’s owned personally, so she prefers to use her phone. 

Goals 

1. Meet new people 

2. Start a study group  

3. Get to know the area around campus 

Pain Points 

1. Needs help figuring out where to start 

2. Unfamiliar with the area 

3. Prefers smaller groups when meeting people 

User 2 

Shiloh, They/Them, 23 

Shiloh is a popular senior English major looking to make the most of their last year on campus by spending as much time outside of class with their friends as they can. They grew up with a home computer and tend to prefer to use their laptop rather than their phone. 

Goals 

1. Hang out with a large variety of friends 

2. Balance hangouts around classes 

3. Schedule a large variety of events 

Pain Points 

1. Has very specific time constraints 

2. Needs to accommodate a large variety of friends 

3. Wants events to be limited to people they already know 

Scenarios: 

1. Create an account - Desktop: 

Shiloh has heard that classmates are using Peer-2-Here to arrange casual gatherings. They open the site on their laptop after finishing an assignment. They want a place to coordinate plans without checking several conversations. On the welcome page, they read the short explanation of the site and choose "Create account" beside "Log in." They recognize that logging in is for people who already have an account. 

Shiloh reviews the labeled username, password, and display name fields together. They read the password requirements before typing, use Tab to move between fields, and follow the visible outline around the current field. They choose a username and password, then accidentally leave the display name blank. When they select "Review account," they read "Enter a display name" beside that field. Their other entries remain, so they add "Shiloh" without starting over. 

Shiloh reads that their display name will identify them to other students in profiles and activity discussions. They think, "I want my classmates to recognize me." They add reading and board games as optional interests, then select "Review account" again. On the review page, they check the spelling of their username, display name, and interests. Their password stays hidden. They use "Edit" to correct an interest and return to the review. 

Once the information looks right, Shiloh selects "Create account." They notice "Creating account..." and wait instead of clicking again. When they read "Account created," followed by a welcome using their display name, they understand that registration succeeded. They arrive at "Explore" and notice the top navigation links for "My activities" and "Create activity." They now know where to look for plans and where to organize one. 

The next evening, Shiloh returns after logging out and chooses "Log in." They mistype their password and read "We could not log you in. Check your username and password." They understand that they have not entered the account yet. They correct the password and try again. When they see their display name and can open "My activities," they know they are back in the same account and can continue planning. 

2. Create an account - Phone: 

Zaria is sitting outside her next classroom when another student mentions Peer-2-Here. She opens the site on her phone because she is more comfortable using it than her new laptop. She wants to meet a few people in her major, but she only has several minutes before class. She reads the welcome message and taps the clearly labeled "Create account" button. 

Zaria reads "Step 1 of 3: Account details" above a single column of labeled fields. She enters a username and uses her phone's password manager to fill a password that meets the stated requirements. She can paste into the password field if needed. After she taps "Continue," she reads that her username is already taken. She adds a few characters and tries again. Her password entry remains, which reassures her that she only needs to fix the username. 

On "Step 2 of 3: Profile," Zaria enters her display name. She reads the explanation that other students will see this name when she joins an activity or posts a question. She notices that interests are optional and chooses "Skip interests for now." She thinks, "I can add those after class. I just want to get started." The large, spaced controls let her make the choice without accidentally selecting a nearby option. 

On "Step 3 of 3: Review," Zaria checks her username and display name. She notices a spelling mistake, taps "Edit profile," corrects it, and returns to the review without retyping her account details. After tapping "Create account," she waits while the button says "Creating account..." She reads "Account created" and a welcome using her corrected name. This tells her that the account exists and she can begin browsing. 

Zaria arrives at "Explore," where she can scroll through one activity card at a time. She notices "Explore," "My activities," and "Create" in the bottom navigation, making it easy to return to those areas with her thumb. She decides to search after class instead of rushing to join something. 

That evening, Zaria returns after logging out. She taps "Log in," uses password autofill, and checks that her display name appears after signing in. She opens her profile and adds her interest in computer science study sessions. She is relieved that skipping interests earlier did not prevent her from using the site or require a second account. 

3. Find and join activity - Desktop: 

Shiloh opens "Explore" on their laptop while checking their personal schedule. They are free on Friday from 3:00 to 5:00 p.m. and want a relaxed gathering. Using the filters beside the results, they choose games, Friday, that time range, and no cost. They select "Apply filters" and check the active filter labels above the list, so they know which restrictions are shaping the results. 

Shiloh compares several cards showing titles, start and end times, locations, costs, and available spaces. One promising activity is labeled "Full," so they move on. They open "Board Games at the Student Center," scheduled from 3:30 to 4:30 p.m. They read the longer description and recognize two names in the participant list. They think, "This gives me time to see friends without making me late." 

Before joining, Shiloh reads the activity's discussion to check whether they need to bring anything. They type "Are games provided, or should I bring one?" in the question field. Beside it, they read that the discussion is visible to everyone who can access the invitation. They are comfortable sharing this question with that audience and select "Post question." They wait through "Posting..." and see their question with their display name and a time, confirming that it was posted. 

Later, Shiloh reopens the activity and reads the organizer's reply that games are provided. They select "Join activity" and review a confirmation containing the title, date, time, and meeting place. They choose "Confirm join." When they read "You have joined this activity," see the "Joined" label, and find the plan under "My activities," they understand that they are counted as a participant. Simply viewing the invitation had not reserved a space. 

On Friday morning, Shiloh learns that their evening commitment has moved earlier. They open "My activities," select the board game gathering, and choose "Leave activity." They read the confirmation explaining that their place will become available to someone else. They select "Confirm leave" because they do not want the organizer to expect them. 

Shiloh reads "You have left this activity" and checks that their name is no longer in the participant list. When they return to their upcoming activities, the gathering is gone. They feel confident that they have withdrawn rather than merely hidden the invitation, and they can look for another gathering when their schedule allows. 

4. Find and join activity - Phone: 

After class, Zaria wants help reviewing introductory programming and would like to meet classmates. She opens "Explore" on her phone and taps "Filters." In the filter panel, she chooses study, today, an evening time range, and no cost. She taps "Show activities" and checks the active filters above the cards. She can remove a filter if her choices are too narrow. 

Zaria scrolls through the cards and opens "Intro Programming Study Group." She reads that it meets from 6:00 to 7:00 p.m. in the campus library and allows four people in total, including the organizer. Two places are available. The small group feels less intimidating than a large gathering. She reads the participant names and the description, which welcomes students who are still learning loops. 

Because Zaria is unfamiliar with campus, she scrolls to the written meeting instructions instead of relying on a building name alone. She reads "Meet inside the library's main entrance by the information desk." In the discussion, she checks whether anyone has already asked what to bring. Finding no answer, she taps "Ask a question" and types "Should I bring my laptop?" She reads that her question will be visible to the invitation's audience, then taps "Post question." Seeing her question appear confirms it was sent. 

Zaria returns after dinner and reads the organizer's reply that a laptop will help but shared notes will also be available. She taps "Join activity" and checks the date, time, and meeting place in the confirmation. After tapping "Confirm join," she reads "You have joined this activity" and sees "Joined" beside the title. She thinks, "They know I am coming now." She opens "My activities" from the bottom navigation and finds the study session there. 

Before leaving her residence hall, Zaria opens the saved plan again. She notices a text label saying "Meeting place updated" and reads the organizer's note explaining that the group will meet at the second-floor tables instead. She reads the directions from the main entrance and understands which details are current. She does not have to interpret a color change or search through old messages to find the new place. 

Zaria brings her laptop and follows the written directions. She knows she can return to the activity discussion if she needs clarification. The clear participant status and updated meeting instructions help her feel prepared to join people she has not met before. 

5. Organize a group activity - Desktop: 

Shiloh wants to spend time with friends on Sunday without extending the gathering into their study time. On their laptop, they choose "Create activity" from the top navigation. They enter "Sunday Board Games," select games as the activity type, and describe a casual gathering in a student center lounge. They intend to meet Sunday from 2:00 to 4:00 p.m. and fill in the date and time fields, then enter no cost and a participant limit of six. They read the note that the limit includes them, leaving five places for guests. 

Shiloh adds the exact meeting spot and writes that games will be provided. In the audience section, they choose "Selected students" because they want this gathering limited to people they know. They search for five registered classmates by username and display name, checking each selected name. They review the explanation that only the selected students and the organizer can view the invitation and its discussion. Receiving the invitation will not automatically count someone as attending. 

Shiloh selects "Review activity" and checks the details and audience together. They notice that they accidentally entered 5:00 p.m. as the end time. They use "Edit details" to correct it to 4:00 p.m., then return to the review. They think, "I need the end time to be clear so everyone can plan around it." They check the five recipients again before selecting "Publish invitation." 

Shiloh waits while they read "Publishing..." and then sees "Invitation published." They open the published activity and confirm its "Selected students" audience and the recipient list. The activity appears under "My activities" in the "Organizing" section. Initially, they see only their own name in the participant list. Later, they reopen it and see classmates who have chosen to join, so they can distinguish invitations from confirmed participants. 

A classmate asks in the discussion whether beginners are welcome. Shiloh opens the question and replies that they will explain the rules. After selecting "Post reply," they see the response beneath the question. They know everyone with access to the invitation can read it, so they do not need to answer the same question separately for each friend. 

On Sunday morning, Shiloh becomes ill and opens the activity's "Manage" options. They choose "Cancel activity," enter a short explanation, and review the warning that current participants will see the cancellation. After selecting "Confirm cancellation," they read "Activity canceled" and check that the explanation and "Canceled" status appear on the plan. They know participants can find the cancellation in "My activities" before leaving, although they cannot assume everyone has read it yet. 

6. Organize a group activity - Phone: 

After enjoying a study session, Zaria wants to organize another one. She is away from her laptop, so she taps "Create" in the phone's bottom navigation. On "Step 1 of 3: Details," she enters "Programming Practice," chooses study as the type, and writes that the group will work through practice problems together. She selects tomorrow, 4:00 to 5:00 p.m., the campus library's first-floor tables, and no cost. 

Zaria adds directions from the main entrance and a note to bring a laptop if possible. She accidentally chooses an end time earlier than the start time. When she taps "Continue," she reads "End time must be after start time" beside the time fields. She corrects the end time and continues. Her title, description, and location remain, so the mistake does not erase her work. 

On "Step 2 of 3: People and audience," Zaria sets the limit to four people. She reads that this includes her, leaving three places for other students. She chooses "Campus community" because she wants to meet classmates beyond the people she already knows. She reads that registered campus users can view the invitation and discussion. She thinks, "Anyone interested can find this, but the group will still stay small." 

On "Step 3 of 3: Review," Zaria scrolls through the complete plan and checks the day, corrected times, meeting instructions, participant limit, and audience. She realizes the description sounds like a formal tutoring session, so she taps "Edit details" and adds that everyone will be learning together. She returns to the review and taps "Publish invitation" once. After "Publishing..." changes to "Invitation published," she finds the activity under "My activities" and its "Organizing" section. 

The next day, Zaria reopens the plan and sees that two students have joined. She reads a participant's question about electrical outlets and answers in the activity discussion. When she arrives early, she finds the first-floor tables occupied. She locates available second-floor tables, opens "Manage," and chooses "Edit activity." She changes the meeting place and adds directions and a short explanation. 

Zaria taps "Review changes" and checks the new location, the previous location, and the notice that participants will have an update in "My activities." She selects "Save changes" and waits for "Activity updated." She reopens the plan and reads the new meeting place beside the "Meeting place updated" label. Seeing one updated activity reassures her that she has changed the existing plan rather than posted a second invitation. She is ready to welcome the group at the new location. 

  

  

Tasks Supported in This Version 

Our three-person group will support the following three user tasks in both interfaces. Posting, discussing, updating, and withdrawing from activities are steps within these tasks. 

Task 1 Create an account or log in 

Action and purpose. A student wants to join the community or return to their existing plans. A new student can choose a username and password, provide a display name, and review the account details before completing registration. Optional interests can be added later. A returning student can log in with their username and password and access the activities they organize or have joined. If required information is missing or credentials are incorrect, the student can understand the problem and try again without unnecessarily repeating completed steps. Account access supports continuity and helps participants recognize the people with whom they are making plans. 

Desktop experience. Students can review the account requirements together and use a keyboard to enter and check their information. They can move through the process using the keyboard alone. This suits a deliberate first visit when they have time to introduce themselves. 

Phone experience. Students can complete the same registration or login process in short, clearly explained steps. They can use password autofill or paste credentials, reducing typing effort and errors on a small screen. They can defer optional profile information and still complete the task. Both interfaces will use the same account requirements and clear success or error feedback. 

Task 2 Find and join a suitable activity 

Action and purpose. A student wants company for an activity that fits their interests, availability, and budget. They can look for suitable invitations by activity type, date, time, or cost, then review a promising plan’s meeting place, duration, available spaces, and participants. They can read existing questions and ask their own before deciding. After choosing to join, they can confirm their participation, return to the current details and discussion, or withdraw if their circumstances change. This task turns a general wish to socialize into a specific plan the student can evaluate and commit to. 

Desktop experience. Students can compare several invitations and review fuller descriptions and conversations with fewer changes of context. This supports planning ahead, such as choosing an activity for a free evening later in the week. They can type a detailed question when they need clarification. 

Phone experience. Students can narrow their choices using the same criteria, then review one activity at a time with its practical details easy to find. Short questions and quick access to joined plans will support use between classes or near the meeting time. They can still read the complete discussion, join, and withdraw. Clear confirmation will help them distinguish browsing an invitation from actually joining it. 

Task 3 Organize an activity with others 

Action and purpose. A student wants to bring people together around a plan. They can describe the activity, enter practical details, set a participant limit, and choose the invitation’s audience. Before publishing, they can review and correct the information. Once the invitation is shared, they can review participants, answer questions, and communicate revised arrangements. If they cancel, they can make that decision clear to participants. This task gives students a way to initiate social opportunities and keep everyone working from the same plan. 

Desktop experience. Organizers can review the activity details and audience together, write longer descriptions, and manage participant questions while planning ahead. The larger workspace will support checking several details before publishing or making a substantial revision. 

Phone experience. Organizers can create the same invitation through shorter stages, with a review of the complete plan before publishing. They can also answer a question or update a meeting place while away from their computer. On either device, they can review who will receive the invitation or update and receive confirmation after publishing changes. These checks help prevent accidental sharing, duplicate posts, and uncertainty about whether participants have access to the revised plan.
